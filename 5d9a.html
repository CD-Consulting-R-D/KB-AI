Who holds the reins? A comparative inventory of LLM agent harnesses in October 2026

A comparative inventory of 73 control components for LLM agents from ten families (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Microsoft, Meta, AWS, NVIDIA, Mistral, open-weight models, open source and standards), on one grid: six types of control, the place of the human, traceability, testing, portability, and seven lessons for a company that deploys agents.

If you are about to let an AI agent act on your systems — read files, run commands, call APIs, send messages — and need to know which controls exist, which are on by default and which you will have to build, this is what the market offered in October 2026, where the vendors converge, and the gaps that none of them closes.

Method: desk research on 21 October 2026 across public documentation, repositories and papers; about 250 pages consulted; 78 sources typed and dated; limits declared in section 10. Nothing was installed or executed.

Start here — if the subject is new to you

A language model on its own only produces text. Once it is connected to tools (a file system, a browser, a mailbox, a database, a payment API), it becomes an agent: it reads, decides and acts. Between the person who asks, the model that decides and the action that follows, every vendor now places a layer of control: rules the model is trained to follow, filters on what goes in and out, permissions and approvals before an action, logs of what happened, test benches to check the whole. That layer is what this entry calls a harness.

It matters because the harness, not the model, is what a company actually configures, audits and answers for. Two deployments of the same model can be safe or reckless depending on how the harness is set.

This entry surveys 73 harness components from ten families (the eight largest model vendors, open-weight models and their tools, and the open-source, academic and standards world), compares them on a common grid, and draws seven lessons for a company that deploys agents.

1. The inventory on one page

The survey covers 73 systems or documents in ten families, after merging duplicates (several tools, such as Meta's LlamaFirewall or NVIDIA's NeMo Guardrails, appear in two families).

Six findings stand out.

  1. Six types of control recur everywhere: behaviour specifications inside the model; input and output guardrails (mostly probabilistic classifiers); permissions and execution hooks (allow, ask or deny rules, approvals, sandboxes); governance of tools and connectors (protocols, gateways, policy engines); traceability (OpenTelemetry almost everywhere); test benches.
  2. The large vendors stack three tiers: the model (trained dispositions), the platform (filters, identity, policy) and the client or SDK (permissions, hooks, approvals). Open-weight models come with a classifier and, since 2025-2026, a command-line agent; infrastructure control is left to whoever deploys them.
  3. Human approval before an action exists in some fifteen systems, always per call or per tool, always opt-in, usually off by default. The 2026 trend is to replace it with a second model that reviews the action, or with a policy engine that sits outside the agent's code.
  4. "Data are not orders" — the principle that a document or a tool result must not be obeyed as an instruction — is handled in four ways: the model's disposition, detection, confinement of effects and, rarely, structural separation. The last is the only robust one and is still experimental. A separate entry will go into this.
  5. No system read documents a signed, timestamped and exportable log of human decisions. The closest are event logs that record who allowed an action (the user, a configuration rule, a hook, a classifier) without signing them.
  6. The field moves in weeks. Between 2025 and October 2026, at least six components surveyed were archived, replaced or abandoned. Any inventory, this one included, has a short shelf life.

2. What counts as a harness, and how the survey was done

Definition. A harness is any device placed between a human, a model and the model's actions to bound what the agent reads, decides, executes and writes. Principles without an implementation, and closed products without verifiable documentation, were left out.

Ten families. OpenAI; Anthropic; Google; Microsoft; Meta; Amazon Web Services; NVIDIA; Mistral; open-weight and small models (Qwen, DeepSeek, Kimi, Gemma, Granite and the tools commonly used with them: Ollama, vLLM, LiteLLM, Goose, Cline, Aider, OpenHands); and a transversal family (open-source libraries, academic benchmarks, standards).

One grid. Each system was described on the same axes: type of control; where it sits (model, platform, client, third-party library, document); place of the human (approval before action: yes, no, opt-in, at what granularity); treatment of instructions hidden in data; traceability; testability; portability (across models, channels and vendors, and the format of the rules); key limit.

Method. Ten desk-research passes, one per family, ran in parallel on 21 October 2026 and read public documentation, repositories and papers only; nothing was installed or executed. Roughly 250 pages were consulted. They were read through an automated summarising tool rather than as raw text: figures (versions, star counts, rates) are those reported on that day and should be checked at the source before being quoted. Section 10 lists what could not be verified.

5. The human in the loop: an exception by design

Approval mechanisms are documented in about fifteen systems: the OpenAI Agents SDK and Codex, Claude Code, Gemini CLI, Google's ADK, Microsoft Agent Framework and Agent Hooks, Bedrock Agents and AgentCore Policy, Strands, Kiro, Mistral connectors and Vibe, Qwen Code, Goose, Cline, OpenHands, LangGraph, NVIDIA OpenShell [S5] [S7] [S11] [S19] [S22] [S26] [S37] [S39] [S41] [S47] [S49] [S53] [S55] [S56] [S69].

Four constants come out of the comparison.

  • Granularity is the call or the tool, never the business decision. A human approves "run this command" or "call this connector", not "send this offer to this client".
  • The default is permissive. "No tool requires approval" by default in Microsoft Agent Framework [S26]; "Autonomous" by default in Goose [S56]; confirmation is something "you can choose to enable" in Bedrock Agents [S37].
  • "Always allow" is remembered by tool name, not by instance, in at least two SDKs: an approval given once can be reused by a different tool that bears the same name [S5] [S26].
  • Without a human present, "ask" becomes "deny" in non-interactive runs (Gemini CLI, the Claude agent SDK) [S22] [S10]. That is the safe choice, and it shows that the approval was the control.

The 2026 trend is to make the human the exception. Several tools now route approvals to a second model by default or on request (a classifier in Claude Code's auto mode, Codex's automatic review, Qwen Code's Auto mode, Goose's Adversary Mode, which fails open) [S7] [S11] [S53] [S57], or let the model assess its own risk (OpenHands) [S55]. The vendors are candid about the limits: Kiro states that its supervised mode "is not a security control" [S42]; Anthropic warns that a limit given in conversation is not a rule [S11].

The other path is to move control outside the agent: a policy engine at a gateway (AgentCore Policy, deny by default, with a log-only mode to rehearse rules before enforcing them) [S38] [S39], a kernel-level sandbox whose policy changes need a human's approval (OpenShell) [S47], or a governance toolkit with formal policy languages (Microsoft AGT) [S29].

6. Traceability: a common format, no evidence-grade log

OpenTelemetry is the common denominator: Claude Code, Codex, the OpenAI Agents SDK, ADK, A2A, AgentCore, NeMo Guardrails, Microsoft Agent Framework all emit, or can emit, OpenTelemetry traces [S6] [S7] [S15] [S23] [S44].

The most useful field for a governance audit is the one Claude Code attaches to each permission decision: its source, among configuration, hook, permanent or temporary user approval, user rejection or abort, and classifier [S15]. It answers the auditor's first question: who, or what, let this action through?

Three gaps remain everywhere.

  • No signature. None of the logs read is signed, so none can serve as evidence on its own. The closest are records without payload and with a monotonic sequence (Agent Hooks) [S28] and a Merkle-tree audit trail announced, not independently verified, by Microsoft's governance toolkit [S29].
  • Content by default. Some tools redact prompts and outputs unless told otherwise; the OpenAI Agents SDK does the opposite and includes sensitive data in traces sent to the vendor by default [S6].
  • Export. Mistral's audit logs cannot be exported [S51]; LiteLLM keeps its audit functions for its enterprise edition [S59].

7. Testing: benchmarks, dry runs and a missing bench

Replayable public benches exist and are model-independent: AgentDojo [S61], InjecAgent [S62], AgentHarm [S63] (which tests malicious requests, not injection), τ-bench [S64], CyberSecEval [S76], garak [S46], PyRIT [S30], OpenAI's IH-Challenge [S3]. Vendor evaluation kits (ADK [S20], Strands, AgentCore) mostly rely on a model as judge.

Dry runs are now standard and are the most practical tool for a company: log-only before enforce (AgentCore Policy) [S38], inspect-only (Model Armor) [S21], detect without blocking (Bedrock Guardrails) [S36], evaluate-only (Agent Hooks) [S28], dry run (FIDES) [S27].

What no bench measures: an agent's fidelity to a written set of instructions in natural language, such as a company's operating rules. τ-bench comes closest, with domain policies and its pass^k metric, which shows compliance falling as the same task is repeated (from 0.46 to 0.225 in the published results) [S64].

8. Portability and maturity

Rule formats fall into four groups: natural language (specifications, rule files, reviewer prompts); configuration (JSON permission rules, TOML in Gemini CLI and Mistral Vibe, YAML in Kiro); formal policy languages (Cedar, Rego, Colang, Invariant's rules) [S40] [S78] [S44] [S67]; and code (SDK callbacks and hooks).

Vendor-independent components are numerous: MCP and A2A as protocols [S16] [S23]; the Anthropic sandbox runtime [S14]; OpenShell [S47]; E2B [S77]; Microsoft's governance toolkit [S29]; Invariant [S67]; LiteLLM [S59]; LangGraph [S69]; the public benches and open classifiers. Cloud-bound components are the platform guardrails and policy engines (Model Armor, Bedrock and AgentCore, Foundry and Entra identity, Mistral's platform).

Maturity is low and turnover high. Within the period covered: AutoGen archived, Semantic Kernel replaced by Agent Framework, LLM Guard archived [S70], Gemini CLI partly superseded, an NVIDIA agentic safety recipe left unmaintained since April 2026, Guardrails AI ending its hosted inference, τ-bench frozen in favour of a successor. Many components are alpha, 0.x or preview (OpenShell, NemoClaw, the Microsoft governance toolkit, Agent Hooks, FIDES, ADK tool confirmation, OpenAI Guardrails).

9. What this means for a company that deploys agents

  1. Assume nothing is on by default. Approvals, sandboxes and audit content are opt-in in most tools. List, for each agent, which controls are actually enabled, and size them to the agent's degree of autonomy (the AWS scoping matrix offers four levels, from no agency to full agency, with controls per level) [S43].
  2. Put the decisive control outside the agent. A rule the agent's own process enforces can be bypassed by whoever controls that process. Gateways with deny-by-default policies, kernel-level sandboxes and separate policy engines hold better than client-side settings.
  3. Approve business effects, not tool calls. Since no tool offers approval at the level of a business decision, design it: route the few actions that commit the company (payments, external messages, deletions, publications) through a confirmation that names the effect.
  4. Treat "always allow" as a standing authorisation. Review persistent approvals like access rights; prefer approvals tied to an instance and an expiry (AgentCore's time-bound policies show it can be done) [S39].
  5. Keep your own decision log. Use OpenTelemetry, keep the source of every permission decision, and add what the vendors do not: timestamps you control, an export, and, where it matters, a signature.
  6. Rehearse before enforcing. Use log-only and inspect-only modes to see what a rule would block before it blocks, and replay a public bench (AgentDojo, garak, PyRIT) on your own configuration, not the vendor's.
  7. Date your inventory. Components appear, change defaults and disappear within months. Re-check versions and defaults at each upgrade.

10. Limits of this inventory

  • Reading method. Pages were read through an automated summariser, not as raw text; version numbers, star counts and rates were not re-checked against the source page. Vendor figures are self-reported unless stated.
  • Not read in full: the complete texts of the OpenAI Model Spec and of Anthropic's constitution; several licences (LlamaFirewall, Llama Guard 4, Kiro, ShieldGemma); the official documentation of Google's enterprise agent platform, Llama Stack safety shields, Kimi Code and the DeepSeek harness repository.
  • Not covered: Amazon Q, Microsoft 365 Copilot, Google's newer coding agents, Mistral's code products, llama.cpp, OpenRouter, Lakera, Rebuff, HarmBench, CrewAI, LlamaIndex, Daytona, Firecracker, OpenAI's gpt-oss-safeguard; public incidents and independent critiques were not searched systematically; the primary texts of ISO/IEC 42001, NIST AI 600-1 and MITRE ATLAS were not read.
  • Shelf life. This is a snapshot of 21 October 2026.
