    Data are not orders: prompt injection in LLM agents, and what actually holds against it (October 2026)

    If you let an AI agent read documents, mail, web pages or tool results and then act on your behalf, and want to know which of the protections you are being sold will hold against someone who tries more than once, this entry ranks them by the level of proof behind them, with the incidents, the numbers and the design rules that follow.

    A language model cannot tell an order from data: instructions and content arrive in one stream, and the skill one buys, following instructions, is what the attacker exploits. Three years of disclosures show the same path, private data plus untrusted content plus an outbound URL, and every probabilistic defence tested adaptively has been bypassed, most at over 90 percent; a residual rate per attempt tends to certainty under retries. The only bounds are architectural: separated flows, deterministic policy outside the agent, operating-system confinement, approval per action, surface reduction. They cost usefulness and do not compose across agents. The vendors say the problem will not be solved; a company should design as if the injection will succeed.

    Does the machine mean it? Intentionality, inference and alignment in large language models (October 2026)

    If you have ever wondered whether an AI "really" wants anything, or whether the alarming experiments about models that deceive their trainers prove more than a clever prompt, this entry gives you the questions a philosopher would ask, the tests an engineer can run, and the honest answer for October 2026: attribution is legitimate, local and revisable; mechanism is within reach for narrow states and out of reach in general; and alignment follows from goal-directedness, not from consciousness.

    Intentionality names two things, aboutness and intention, and the analytic tradition (Anscombe, Davidson, Searle, Dennett, Bratman) gives different verdicts for a machine. Three tests make attribution empirical: a predictive gain, a faithful answer to "why?", a cause located by intervention, plus a persistent plan. The 2024-2026 experiments on alignment faking, scheming and agentic misalignment pass the first test and fail the third. Alignment follows from goal-directedness, not from consciousness: one does not harness what does not pull.

    Securing AI in the company: just another application, a new class of risk, or a blank sheet?

    If you run or advise an organisation that uses AI tools, or are about to, this is where the existing security rules stop, what a language model changes that no firewall sees, and the six steps that cost almost nothing.

    Neither camp is right alone. The classical security baseline (access, segmentation, patching, secrets, logging, suppliers) remains mandatory and is often missing on AI stacks: 97 % of organisations breached through an AI system had no access control on it. AI then adds a class of risk those controls do not see: a language model does not separate instructions from data, so prompt injection has no patch; injections persist in memories, indexes and weights; agents act in the user's name and treat instructions as suggestions; the company answers for what its chatbot says. The advanced defences are returns to fundamentals applied to an untrusted component. What help from AI is proven to deliver is narrower than what is sold; patch management comes first. Belgian and European rules in four dates, fifteen incidents, twenty-two measures by priority.
